# The Fixed-Support Dickinson Certificate Complex

## Status And Purpose

This is a mathematical research note about choosing several positive right-hand sides for one nonsingular Dickinson support. It
identifies the structure formed by all resulting upper endpoints, explains what can and cannot be enumerated efficiently, and gives
theoretical guardrails for future experiments.

The central conclusion is:

> For one processed support, the supports certified by all admissible positive right-hand sides form a translated simplicial
> complex. Its maximal faces are the incomparable upper endpoints worth retaining. The complex is generated by a low-dimensional
> hyperplane arrangement, while its missing faces are small Helly--Farkas conflicts.

This structure does not make the general optimization easy. It does, however, tell us exactly what the search is looking for, when
complete enumeration is reasonable, what constitutes a proof of completeness, and when an experiment must stop without making a
completeness claim.

The underlying interval certificate is Peter J. C. Dickinson's Theorem 4.6 in “A New Certificate for Copositivity,” *Linear
Algebra and its Applications* 569 (2019), 15--37, DOI
[`10.1016/j.laa.2018.12.025`](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.laa.2018.12.025). The application of convex nerves, Helly theory,
hyperplane arrangements, and infeasible-subsystem hypergraphs to the positive-right-hand-side family is coposit research.

Related local notes are:

- [`AFFINE_RHS_DICKINSON_CERTIFICATE_GEOMETRY.md`](AFFINE_RHS_DICKINSON_CERTIFICATE_GEOMETRY.md), for the exact relation between
  the lower endpoint, upper endpoint, and number of free indices;
- [`ORDINARY_DICKINSON_CERTIFICATE_ENGINE_RESEARCH.md`](ORDINARY_DICKINSON_CERTIFICATE_ENGINE_RESEARCH.md), for activation loss and
  bounded Dickinson intervals; and

  intervals dominate the computation.

## Mathematical Origin And Terminology

This construction does not come from one isolated branch of mathematics. It starts with a copositivity certificate and becomes a
finite set-system problem whose geometry is controlled by linear inequalities. The relevant areas are the following.

### Copositive optimization

The starting point is Dickinson's interval certificate. A vector $u$ defines a lower support $L(u)$ and an upper support $U(u)$,
and certifies every principal support between them. In this note, one nonsingular principal matrix $A_I$ is fixed while the positive
right-hand side in $A_Ix=b$ varies. The resulting family of Dickinson intervals is the object being studied. The interval theorem
comes from Dickinson; the analysis of the whole positive-right-hand-side family is a coposit-specific synthesis developed here.
The primary copositivity source is Reference 1.

### Boolean lattices and extremal set theory

All supports form the Boolean lattice $2^{[n]}$, ordered by inclusion. A Dickinson certificate is an interval in that lattice. For
one fixed root $I$, the attainable outside-index sets form a downward-closed family, also called an **abstract simplicial complex**.
Its inclusion-maximal members are its **facets**. Because a smaller attainable outside set is already contained in a larger one, the
facets form an antichain and are the only upper endpoints that matter for anchored coverage.

### Convex geometry and linear inequalities

After normalizing $b$ to a simplex, each outside condition $(Au)_j\geq0$ is one closed halfspace condition in the right-hand-side
variables. A set of outside indices is attainable exactly when the corresponding halfspaces have a common point in that simplex.
This gives two different classical tools:

- **Helly's theorem** bounds the size of a smallest infeasible collection of halfspaces; and
- **Farkas' lemma** supplies an algebraic certificate proving that such a collection is infeasible.

Helly explains why a conflict has a small witness when the effective dimension is small. Farkas explains how to verify that witness
exactly. They are complementary results, not two names for the same argument.
The original sources are References 2 and 3.

### Hyperplane arrangements and oriented matroids

The equations $(Au)_j=0$ cut the normalized right-hand-side simplex into cells. Within one cell, every sign is fixed, so the upper
endpoint $U(u)$ is fixed as well. This is a **hyperplane arrangement** restricted to a convex polytope. Its sign patterns are
restrictions of the **covectors** studied in oriented-matroid theory. Arrangement theory therefore explains why the search is finite
and why complete enumeration is practical only in low effective dimension. We use this language for structure; the present note
does not require the full oriented-matroid machinery.
References 4, 5, and 11 give the relevant arrangement and oriented-matroid background.

### Combinatorial topology

The attainable outside sets form the **nerve** of the convex halfspace sections: a subset is present precisely when the associated
convex sets have nonempty common intersection. A nerve of convex sets in $r$ dimensions is called $r$-representable. Standard
results imply additional restrictions such as $r$-Leray behavior, while Wegner's theorem gives $r$-collapsibility for nerves of
convex sets in Euclidean $r$-space. These facts fence the possible complexes more tightly than the statement “downward closed,” but
they do not prevent exponentially many facets.
References 6 and 7 cover convex nerves, collapsibility, and their combinatorial consequences.

### Mathematical optimization and infeasibility analysis

Choosing as many simultaneously feasible outside inequalities as possible is a **maximum feasible subsystem** problem (MaxFS).
Its minimal infeasible subsystems are **irreducible infeasible subsystems** (IISs). If every IIS is stored as a hyperedge, then an
attainable outside set is exactly an independent set of that hypergraph, and a facet is a maximal independent set. Equivalently,
the omitted outside indices form a hitting set of all IIS hyperedges. This is the established optimization language behind the
conflict-guided and branch-and-cut algorithms proposed later in the note.
References 8--10 develop MaxFS complexity, IIS hypergraphs, and branch-and-cut algorithms.

### Which names and results are local

The terms *simplicial complex*, *nerve*, *facet*, *hyperplane arrangement*, *covector*, *Helly theorem*, *Farkas certificate*,
*MaxFS*, and *IIS hypergraph* are established terminology. **Fixed-support Dickinson certificate complex** is a descriptive name
introduced in this note for the particular complex generated by varying $b$ at one Dickinson root. **Helly--Farkas conflict** is
likewise shorthand for a small infeasible subsystem whose size is controlled by Helly and whose infeasibility is certified by
Farkas.

The individual ingredients are classical. Their fixed-support combination and the proposed use of its facet antichain for wider
copositivity pruning are derived here. This note does not claim that the synthesis is globally novel; establishing novelty would
require a dedicated literature review beyond the references collected below.

## 1. Why Certificate Coverage Is The Central Quantity

Dickinson's method visits a principal support, performs an exact factorization, and obtains a certificate that removes an interval
of the Boolean support lattice. The expensive part is not merely visiting one support. The decisive question is how many later
supports that one exact calculation prevents us from visiting.

The experiments with ordinary, SAT-backed, decision-diagram-backed, right-hand-side, and KKT-guided variants repeatedly exposed the
same practical distinction:

- a wide certificate can replace an enormous number of later exact principal solves;
- a narrow certificate saves little and may hide a later support that would have produced a much wider certificate; and
- improving enumeration overhead cannot compensate indefinitely for certificates that prune only a small part of the lattice.

This motivates a focused problem:

> After factoring one principal matrix, can we cheaply obtain several genuinely different Dickinson intervals whose union covers
> substantially more of the support lattice than the single interval selected by the present right-hand-side search?

The requirement “cheaply” is essential. A stronger certificate search has no value if finding it costs as much as processing the
supports it would remove. The theory below therefore serves as a fence around the optimization: it identifies small cases that admit
complete exact treatment, compact proof objects that can guide larger searches, and explicit conditions under which a bounded
search remains only heuristic.

## 2. Dickinson Intervals From A Fixed Principal Support

Let $A\in\mathbb Z^{n\times n}$ be symmetric. For a vector $u\in\mathbb R^n$, define

$$
L(u)=\operatorname{supp}(u)=\{i:u_i\neq0\}
$$

and

$$
U(u)=N_A(u)=\{j:(Au)_j\geq0\}.
$$

If $u$ has a positive coordinate and $L(u)\subseteq U(u)$, Dickinson's theorem certifies every support in

$$
[L(u),U(u)]
=
\{J:L(u)\subseteq J\subseteq U(u)\}.
$$

Now fix a processed support $I\subseteq[n]$, write $k=|I|$, and assume that the principal matrix $A_I$ is nonsingular. Choose a
strictly positive right-hand side $b\in\mathbb R^k$, solve

$$
A_Ix=b,
$$

and embed $x$ in $u\in\mathbb R^n$ by placing zeros outside $I$. Then

$$
(Au)_I=b>0,
$$

so

$$
L(u)\subseteq I\subseteq U(u).
$$

Even when $x$ contains zeros and $L(u)\subsetneq I$, the anchored interval

$$
[I,U(u)]
$$

is contained in the full Dickinson interval $[L(u),U(u)]$. We first study this common anchored part because it is controlled
entirely by the upper endpoint. Any reduction of $L(u)$ supplies additional coverage below $I$ and can be added afterwards.

### 2.1 Why the positive-coordinate condition does not spoil the hard positive case

The convex construction below varies $b$ over a positive simplex. A Dickinson vector must still contain a positive coordinate.
Suppose instead that $x=A_I^{-1}b\leq0$. Then $y=-x\geq0$, $y\neq0$, and

$$
y^TA_Iy=x^TA_Ix=x^Tb<0.
$$

Thus $y$ is an exact non-copositivity witness for $A_I$. Consequently:

- when the matrix remains on the copositive branch, every positive-right-hand-side solution is certificate-admissible; and
- if a nonpositive solution appears, it is stronger than a pruning certificate because it ends the copositivity decision.

The certificate complex below therefore describes the expensive positive branch exactly. A negative witness remains a separate
terminal outcome.

## 3. Normalize The Right-Hand-Side Domain

Positive scaling of $b$ scales $x$ and $Au$ without changing $L(u)$ or $U(u)$. The numerical MILP experiment uses the
compact positive simplex

$$
B_\varepsilon
=
\left\{
b\in\mathbb R^k:
b_i\geq\varepsilon,
\quad
\mathbf1^Tb=1
\right\},
$$

where $0<\varepsilon<1/k$. This set has affine dimension $k-1$.

Let

$$
O=[n]\setminus I,
\qquad
m=|O|=n-k,
$$

and define the outside-product matrix

$$
P=A_{O,I}A_I^{-1}\in\mathbb R^{m\times k}.
$$

For $j\in O$, write $p_j^T$ for the corresponding row of $P$. The outside product is

$$
(Au)_j=p_j^Tb.
$$

Hence every outside index defines a convex set of admissible right-hand sides:

$$
C_j
=
\{b\in B_\varepsilon:p_j^Tb\geq0\}.
$$

Each $C_j$ is the intersection of the simplex with one closed halfspace.

The fixed $\varepsilon$ matters. Every completeness statement below is complete relative to $B_\varepsilon$. It is not
automatically a completeness statement for the entire open domain $b>0$. Removing that restriction requires treating arbitrarily
small positive coordinates and boundary limits separately.

## 4. The Fixed-Support Certificate Complex

Define

$$
\mathcal K_I
=
\left\{
T\subseteq O:
\bigcap_{j\in T}C_j\neq\varnothing
\right\}.
$$

Thus $T\in\mathcal K_I$ means that one admissible right-hand side makes all outside products indexed by $T$ nonnegative at the
same time. The same right-hand side may make additional outside products nonnegative as well.

### Theorem 4.1: the attainable anchored coverage is a simplicial complex

The family $\mathcal K_I$ is an abstract simplicial complex: whenever $T\in\mathcal K_I$ and $R\subseteq T$, then
$R\in\mathcal K_I$. Moreover,

$$
\boxed{
\bigcup_{b\in B_\varepsilon}[I,U(b)]
=
\{I\cup T:T\in\mathcal K_I\}.
}
$$

#### Proof

If $T\in\mathcal K_I$, there is a $b\in B_\varepsilon$ belonging to every $C_j$ with $j\in T$. The same $b$ belongs to
every $C_j$ with $j\in R\subseteq T$, so $R\in\mathcal K_I$. This proves downward closure.

For the displayed identity, first take $J\in[I,U(b)]$ and set $T=J\setminus I$. Every $j\in T$ satisfies $p_j^Tb\geq0$, so
$b\in\bigcap_{j\in T}C_j$ and $T\in\mathcal K_I$.

Conversely, if $T\in\mathcal K_I$, choose $b\in\bigcap_{j\in T}C_j$. Then $I\cup T\subseteq U(b)$, and hence
$I\cup T\in[I,U(b)]$. $\square$

The complex $\mathcal K_I$ is the exact combinatorial object behind the union of upper-endpoint certificates from one principal
factorization. The translation by $I$ only restores the indices that are fixed in every anchored interval.

### Corollary 4.2: cardinality coverage is the $f$-vector

Let $f_q$ be the number of $q$-element faces of $\mathcal K_I$. Then $f_q$ is exactly the number of supports of cardinality
$k+q$ covered by the union of anchored intervals.

This gives a direct interpretation to the usual per-cardinality diagnostics: they measure the $f$-vector of the part of the
certificate complex discovered so far.

## 5. Only Maximal Upper Endpoints Matter

A facet of a simplicial complex is a face that is maximal under set inclusion.

### Theorem 5.1: facets are precisely the useful incomparable upper endpoints

Let $F\subseteq O$. The following statements are equivalent:

1. $F$ is a facet of $\mathcal K_I$.
2. There is a $b\in B_\varepsilon$ whose outside nonnegative set is exactly $F$, and no attainable outside nonnegative set
   strictly contains $F$.
3. The anchored interval $[I,I\cup F]$ is not contained in another anchored interval obtainable from $B_\varepsilon$.

#### Proof

If $F$ is a facet, choose $b\in\bigcap_{j\in F}C_j$. If the same $b$ also satisfied an outside index $s\notin F$, then
$F\cup\{s\}\in\mathcal K_I$, contradicting maximality. Thus its exact outside nonnegative set is $F$. The remaining
equivalences follow from

$$
[I,I\cup F_1]\subseteq[I,I\cup F_2]
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
F_1\subseteq F_2.
\qquad\square
$$

Therefore the complete union has the irredundant representation

$$
\bigcup_{b\in B_\varepsilon}[I,U(b)]
=
\bigcup_{F\in\operatorname{Facets}(\mathcal K_I)}[I,I\cup F].
$$

This is the first algorithmic guardrail:

> Search only for facets. Every nonmaximal upper endpoint is already subsumed.

The facets need not be nested. They form an antichain under inclusion.

### Example 5.2: one root gives two incomparable intervals

Consider

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&1&-2\\
0&1&-2&1\\
1&-2&1&0\\
-2&1&0&1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
I=\{1,2\}.
$$

Here $A_I$ is the identity. With $b^{(1)}=(3,1)^T$,

$$
Au^{(1)}=(3,1,1,-5)^T,
$$

so the interval is

$$
[\{1,2\},\{1,2,3\}].
$$

With $b^{(2)}=(1,3)^T$,

$$
Au^{(2)}=(1,3,-5,1)^T,
$$

so the interval is

$$
[\{1,2\},\{1,2,4\}].
$$

Scaling either right-hand side to have coordinate sum one does not change its interval. Thus both candidates belong to
$B_\varepsilon$ whenever $\varepsilon\leq1/4$.

Neither interval contains the other. Together they certify

$$
\{\{1,2\},\{1,2,3\},\{1,2,4\}\},
$$

whereas either one alone certifies only two supports. In outside coordinates, the complex has facets $\{3\}$ and $\{4\}$, while
$\{3,4\}$ is a missing face.

This example proves that maximizing one value of $|U|$ is not the same as maximizing total coverage obtained from several
right-hand sides.

## 6. Hyperplane-Arrangement Structure

The equations

$$
p_j^Tb=0,
\qquad j\in O,
$$

form an affine hyperplane arrangement inside $B_\varepsilon$. Within the relative interior of one arrangement cell, the sign of
every outside product is fixed. The upper endpoint changes only when $b$ crosses one of these hyperplanes. Points on the
hyperplanes must also be retained because equality counts as nonnegative and may produce an upper endpoint larger than either
adjacent open cell.

In the language of oriented matroids, these sign patterns are realizable covectors of the homogenized arrangement, restricted to
the positive simplex. The oriented-matroid language is descriptive rather than required by the algorithm; the practical facts are
that the sign patterns are finite, adjacent cells differ by controlled sign changes, and the dimension of the arrangement governs
its worst-case size.

Hyperplane-arrangement region counting and its extension to topological hyperplanes are treated by Forge and Zaslavsky in
“On the Division of Space by Topological Hyperplanes,”
[`arXiv:math/0609047`](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0609047).

### 6.1 Effective dimension

Let $Q\in\mathbb R^{k\times(k-1)}$ have columns spanning the tangent space

$$
\{\delta\in\mathbb R^k:\mathbf1^T\delta=0\}.
$$

Define

$$
r=\operatorname{rank}(PQ)\leq k-1.
$$

The affine image $P(B_\varepsilon)$ has dimension $r$. All outside signs depend only on a point in this $r$-dimensional image,
even when $k-1$ is larger. Thus $r$, not merely $k$, is the correct structural parameter.

For fixed $r$, an arrangement of $m$ hyperplanes has $O(m^r)$ total combinatorial complexity. This gives the second guardrail:

> Complete sign-pattern enumeration is reasonable only when the effective dimension $r$ is small.

The lowest-dimensional cases are especially simple:

- **$r=0$.** All outside products are constant over $B_\varepsilon$. There is one upper endpoint.
- **$r=1$.** Each outside product is affine in one scalar and changes sign at most once. Sort the exact breakpoints and sweep the
  line. Evaluating every open segment and every breakpoint enumerates all upper endpoints in $O(m\log m)$ time after the products
  are known.
- **$r=2$.** The problem is a line arrangement in a convex polygon. There are $O(m^2)$ cells, edges, and vertices in the generic
  worst case. Complete enumeration is possible for moderate $m$, but requires a real arrangement implementation.
- **Larger fixed $r$.** The complexity remains polynomial in $m$ for fixed $r$, but the exponent is $r$. Once $r$ grows
  with $k$, this is no longer a useful guarantee.

Rows of $P$ that are positive scalar multiples define the same halfspace and always switch together. They may be represented by
one weighted condition. Zero rows are always in the upper endpoint. These exact reductions can lower the effective number of
hyperplanes before any optimization begins.

## 7. Convex Nerves And Helly's Theorem

The complex $\mathcal K_I$ is the nerve of the convex family $\{C_j:j\in O\}$: a set of vertices forms a face exactly when the
corresponding convex sets have a common point. Those preimages lie in the $(k-1)$-dimensional simplex. To obtain the sharper
effective-dimension statement, define

$$
D_j
=
P(B_\varepsilon)\cap\{y\in\mathbb R^m:y_j\geq0\}.
$$

The family $\{D_j:j\in O\}$ has the same nerve because an intersection of the $C_j$ is nonempty exactly when the corresponding
intersection of the $D_j$ is nonempty. The $D_j$ are convex subsets of the $r$-dimensional affine image $P(B_\varepsilon)$.
Therefore $\mathcal K_I$ is an $r$-representable simplicial complex.

This connects the right-hand-side problem to the theory of convex intersection patterns. A readable overview is Martin Tancer's
“Intersection Patterns of Convex Sets via Simplicial Complexes,”
[`arXiv:1102.0417`](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.0417).

### Theorem 7.1: every minimal conflict is small

Suppose $T\subseteq O$ is infeasible:

$$
\bigcap_{j\in T}C_j=\varnothing.
$$

Then there is a subset $R\subseteq T$ satisfying

$$
|R|\leq r+1
$$

and

$$
\bigcap_{j\in R}C_j=\varnothing.
$$

In particular, every inclusion-minimal nonface of $\mathcal K_I$ has at most $r+1$ vertices.

#### Proof

The equivalent sets $D_j$ are convex subsets of an affine space of dimension $r$. Helly's theorem states that if a finite family
of convex sets in $\mathbb R^r$ has empty intersection, some subfamily of at most $r+1$ members already has empty intersection.
Apply the theorem to $\{D_j:j\in T\}$, and transfer the resulting empty intersection back to the corresponding $C_j$.
$\square$

This is stronger than merely learning that a large proposed upper endpoint is infeasible. It says that infeasibility always has a
small combinatorial reason. A rational Farkas certificate can verify the corresponding contradiction exactly.

If $R$ is such a conflict and $z_j$ indicates that outside index $j$ is requested, the conflict becomes the valid cut

$$
\sum_{j\in R}z_j\leq |R|-1.
$$

The cuts have size at most $r+1$, regardless of $m=n-k$.

The nerve has further known structure: every $r$-representable complex is $r$-collapsible and $r$-Leray. Informally, it admits
a sequence of bounded-dimensional elementary collapses and has no induced topological holes in dimensions $r$ and above. These
facts constrain the complex, but they do not by themselves provide a cheap facet-enumeration algorithm. They should not be turned
into implementation machinery until a concrete use is demonstrated.

## 8. The Irreducible-Infeasible-Subsystem Hypergraph

Let $\mathcal H_I$ be the family of all inclusion-minimal conflicts:

$$
\mathcal H_I
=
\left\{
R\subseteq O:
\bigcap_{j\in R}C_j=\varnothing,
\quad
\bigcap_{j\in R\setminus\{s\}}C_j\neq\varnothing
\text{ for every }s\in R
\right\}.
$$

Regard $\mathcal H_I$ as a hypergraph on the outside indices. Its hyperedges are the irreducible infeasible subsystems, usually
abbreviated IISs, relative to the hard domain $B_\varepsilon$.

Then

$$
T\in\mathcal K_I
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
T\text{ contains no hyperedge of }\mathcal H_I.
$$

Consequently:

- faces of $\mathcal K_I$ are independent sets of $\mathcal H_I$;
- facets of $\mathcal K_I$ are maximal independent sets;
- the complement of a facet is a minimal hitting set of $\mathcal H_I$; and
- a maximum-cardinality facet is the complement of a minimum-cardinality hitting set.

This is the established structural connection between maximum feasible subsystems and IIS hypergraphs. See Amaldi, Pfetsch, and
Trotter, “On the Maximum Feasible Subsystem Problem, IISs and IIS-Hypergraphs,” *Mathematical Programming* 95 (2003), 533--554,
[`author PDF`](https://www2.mathematik.tu-darmstadt.de/~pfetsch/Publications/maxfs.pdf).

The correspondence also identifies the hard boundary. Finding a maximum feasible subsystem is NP-hard in general, even under
strong restrictions on the linear relations; see Amaldi and Kann, “The Complexity and Approximability of Finding Maximum Feasible
Subsystems of Linear Relations,” *Theoretical Computer Science* 147 (1995), 181--210,
[`DOI 10.1016/0304-3975(94)00254-G`](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975(94)00254-G).

Helly's theorem bounds the size of every hyperedge by $r+1$, but this does not bound the number of hyperedges or facets. Even a
hypergraph containing only two-element edges can encode a hard maximum-independent-set problem. Therefore no theorem here justifies
unrestricted facet enumeration for large $r$.

## 9. MILP Formulation For One Facet

For completeness, this section records the bounded MILP used to search for one large upper endpoint.

### Given data

- the right-hand-side domain $B_\varepsilon$;
- the outside-product rows $p_j^T$, $j\in O$; and
- one binary variable $z_j$ for every outside index.

The intended meaning is that $z_j=1$ requests the inequality $p_j^Tb\geq0$.

### Tight big-$M$ constants on the positive simplex

For one row $p_j$, the minimum over $B_\varepsilon$ is

$$
\min_{b\in B_\varepsilon}p_j^Tb
=
\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^k p_{js}
+
(1-k\varepsilon)\min_{1\leq s\leq k}p_{js}.
$$

Indeed, the mandatory mass $\varepsilon$ is placed on every coordinate and all remaining mass is placed on a smallest coefficient.
Therefore the tight nonnegative deactivation constant is

$$
M_j
=
\max\left(
0,
-\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^k p_{js}
-(1-k\varepsilon)\min_s p_{js}
\right).
$$

No auxiliary LP is required to compute $M_j$.

### Optimization problem

The MILP is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max\quad & \sum_{j\in O}z_j,\\
\text{subject to}\quad
& b_s\geq\varepsilon && (s=1,\ldots,k),\\
& \mathbf1^Tb=1,\\
& p_j^Tb\geq-M_j(1-z_j) && (j\in O),\\
& z_j\in\{0,1\} && (j\in O).
\end{aligned}
$$

If positive-proportional rows have been grouped, the objective uses the group cardinality as the weight of its binary variable.

At an optimal solution, every exactly nonnegative outside row can be marked with $z_j=1$: doing so preserves feasibility and
improves or preserves every monotone dominance constraint. The exact upper endpoint must nevertheless be reconstructed from the
exact product $Pb$; floating signs are proposals, not certificate evidence.

## 10. Enumerating Facets With Dominance Cuts

Suppose the first exactly verified facet is $F_1\subseteq O$. To exclude $F_1$ and every subset already dominated by it, add

$$
\sum_{j\notin F_1}z_j\geq1.
$$

Any later solution must contain at least one outside index absent from $F_1$. After obtaining facets
$F_1,\ldots,F_t$, retain every cut

$$
\sum_{j\notin F_\ell}z_j\geq1,
\qquad
\ell=1,\ldots,t.
$$

### Theorem 10.1: repeated exact optimization enumerates the facets

Assume every MILP is solved exactly to global optimality and every endpoint is reconstructed exactly. Then each returned endpoint
is a new facet of $\mathcal K_I$. When the MILP with all dominance cuts becomes infeasible, the retained endpoints are all facets
of $\mathcal K_I$.

#### Proof

Every strict superset of a solution satisfies all existing dominance cuts because those cuts are monotone under adding indices. If
a returned endpoint had an attainable strict superset, that superset would have a larger objective value, contradicting optimality.
Thus every returned endpoint is a facet.

When the residual MILP is infeasible, every attainable endpoint violates at least one dominance cut. Violating the cut belonging to
$F_\ell$ means that it contains no index outside $F_\ell$, hence it is a subset of $F_\ell$. Therefore every attainable
endpoint is dominated by a retained facet. $\square$

This theorem supplies an exact stopping certificate. A time limit, node limit, floating infeasibility result, or failed exact
reconstruction does not. Under those outcomes, the result is only a partial antichain.

## 11. Measure Marginal Coverage, Not Only Upper Cardinality

Let $F_1,\ldots,F_t\subseteq O$ be retained facets. The anchored interval belonging to $F_\ell$ contains $2^{|F_\ell|}$
supports. Their intersections remain Boolean intervals because

$$
[I,I\cup F_a]\cap[I,I\cup F_b]
=
[I,I\cup(F_a\cap F_b)].
$$

Hence the exact total anchored coverage is

$$
\left|
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^t[I,I\cup F_\ell]
\right|
=
\sum_{\varnothing\neq R\subseteq[t]}
(-1)^{|R|+1}
2^{\left|\bigcap_{\ell\in R}F_\ell\right|}.
$$

The exact coverage at cardinality $k+q$ is

$$
\sum_{\varnothing\neq R\subseteq[t]}
(-1)^{|R|+1}
\binom{\left|\bigcap_{\ell\in R}F_\ell\right|}{q}.
$$

For a new facet $F$, its marginal contribution beyond the retained facets is

$$
\Delta(F)
=
\sum_{R\subseteq[t]}
(-1)^{|R|}
2^{\left|F\cap\bigcap_{\ell\in R}F_\ell\right|},
$$

where $R=\varnothing$ contributes $2^{|F|}$.

When $t\leq8$, these formulas require at most $2^8=256$ packed-bitset intersections. This is small compared with exact principal
factorization. A bounded experiment should therefore rank or stop candidates by exact marginal coverage $\Delta(F)$, not only by
$|F|$ or $|U|$.

The full Dickinson interval can be larger when $L(u)\subsetneq I$. Such extra downward coverage must be evaluated with the actual
pair $(L,U)$. The anchored formula remains a guaranteed lower bound and allows fair comparison among candidates from the same root.

## 12. Practical Theoretical Guardrails

The theory suggests the following staged search. This is a research design, not yet a claim that every stage will pay for itself in
runtime.

### Stage 1: keep information already produced by Rays

The existing Halfspace-Rays search evaluates several candidate right-hand sides but retains only one lexicographic winner. Collect
the nondominated upper endpoints encountered by those existing exact sweeps. Keep compact support bitsets during subsumption and
materialize full exact vectors only for the small set of finalists when the current implementation has deferred that work.

This is the cheapest first experiment because it reuses the factorization, direction products, and breakpoint sweeps already paid
for.

### Stage 2: use the effective dimension as a hard gate

- $r=0$: return the unique endpoint.
- $r=1$: enumerate the complete arrangement exactly by a breakpoint sweep.
- $r=2$: consider complete planar enumeration only when $m$ is moderate.
- larger $r$: do not attempt complete arrangement enumeration by default.

Computing an expensive exact rank solely to avoid a small optimization would defeat this gate. Use $k-1$ as the safe upper bound
unless $P$ is already materialized in an exact form that makes rank reduction cheap.

### Stage 3: discover small conflicts on demand

For larger $r$, let a binary master problem propose an outside set. Test its halfspace feasibility by LP. If it is infeasible,
extract an irreducible conflict and verify it by an exact Farkas certificate. Add the resulting cut of size at most $r+1$.

This branch-and-cut view avoids solving or storing every possible conflict in advance. It follows the established MaxFS/IIS
approach; an exact branch-and-cut treatment is given by Pfetsch, “Branch-and-Cut for the Maximum Feasible Subsystem Problem,”
*SIAM Journal on Optimization* 19 (2008), 21--38,
[`DOI 10.1137/050645828`](https://doi.org/10.1137/050645828).

### Stage 4: retain a bounded facet antichain

Keep only exactly verified, pairwise incomparable endpoints. An initial experimental cap such as four or eight facets is a resource
policy, not a theorem. Stop adding facets when their exact marginal coverage is too small to plausibly repay another optimization.

### Stage 5: distinguish three stopping outcomes

1. **Complete:** the exact residual problem is infeasible after all dominance cuts. Every attainable endpoint is dominated.
2. **Bounded partial result:** the facet cap, time limit, node limit, or marginal-coverage threshold is reached. The retained
   intervals are valid, but the antichain may be incomplete.
3. **Negative witness:** some admissible positive right-hand side produces $A_I^{-1}b\leq0$. Copositivity fails and no further
   certificate search is needed.

No silent fallback may turn the second outcome into the first.

## 13. What The Theory Proves And What It Does Not

### Proved for the fixed support

- The union of all anchored intervals is a translated simplicial complex.
- Its facets are exactly the incomparable maximal upper endpoints.
- It is the nerve of convex halfspace sections of the positive simplex.
- Its effective dimension is at most $k-1$, and can be smaller when the outside products have lower affine rank.
- Every minimal infeasible outside set has at most $r+1$ indices.
- The minimal infeasible sets form an IIS hypergraph; facets are its maximal independent sets.
- Repeated exact optimization with dominance cuts enumerates every facet and proves completion by residual infeasibility.
- The union and per-cardinality coverage of a bounded facet family can be counted exactly by inclusion--exclusion.

### Established limitations

- Maximum feasible subsystem is NP-hard in general.
- Small Helly conflicts do not imply that there are few conflicts or few facets.
- The number of facets can be exponential when the effective dimension grows.
- The complex is not generally a matroid, so greedy exchange arguments do not apply.
- Completeness within the fixed $B_\varepsilon$ does not imply completeness over every strictly positive right-hand side.

### Open algorithmic questions

1. How many nondominated endpoints does the current Rays search already encounter on the hard BPQY and Burer matrices?
2. Does exact marginal coverage from two to eight Rays endpoints repay the extra SAT clauses?
3. How often is the effective rank $r$ substantially smaller than $k-1$?
4. Do structured difficult matrices produce many repeated or positive-proportional outside rows?
5. Can the existing small LP solver return useful exact-reconstructible Farkas conflicts cheaply enough for conflict-guided search?
6. At what $r$, $m$, and incumbent violation count does a second facet cease to repay its optimization cost?
7. Can boundary right-hand sides outside the fixed $B_\varepsilon$ provide materially wider exact certificates without creating a
   numerically fragile search?

These questions should be answered by a focused diagnostic experiment before creating another maintained solver model.

## 14. Main Practical Conclusion

The right-hand-side search should not be described as “find the best vector $b$.” That wording hides the actual combinatorics.
The correct problem is:

> From one exact principal factorization, find a small collection of incomparable maximal upper endpoints whose interval union has
> large exact marginal coverage.

The theoretical fence is now explicit:

- hyperplane-arrangement dimension controls when complete enumeration is plausible;
- Helly and Farkas provide small exact conflict certificates;
- the IIS hypergraph explains both facet discovery and worst-case hardness;
- dominance cuts define an exact completion test; and
- inclusion--exclusion measures the actual lattice coverage bought by each additional endpoint.

This leaves room for useful experiments without pretending that an unrestricted sequence of MILPs can be made harmless. The first
experiment should remain the cheapest one: retain a bounded antichain of nondominated endpoints that the existing Rays machinery
already computes, and measure their exact marginal coverage before adding a more expensive optimizer.
